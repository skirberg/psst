The bet

Sell moves, at the right moment.

Every guide sells places. Places are a commodity: Google has all of them. What nobody gives you is the move: the right place, at the right hour, in today's weather, with exactly what to order or who to ask for, next to whatever is happening in the city that week. That's what a friend who lives here texts you. That's the product.

Right timeEvery recommendation knows the hour, the weather, sunset and what's on tonight.
The exact moveWhat to order, where to sit, how to get in. Names alone are useless.
One city, deepMiami first, picked by locals with taste. Built so any city can follow.
Made to sharePlans for a group, a link that works on any phone, no app to install.

Strategy

Why anyone would use us over Google.

I looked at who already does this. The category is real and crowded, so the edge has to be sharp. Here's what each player is good at and the gap we take.

Google MapsEvery place, hours, directionsRanks by review volume, so the mainstream wins. No taste, no timing.
TikTok, InstagramDiscovery, vibes, what's newChaotic, unsearchable, and a viral spot is packed by the time you go. No plan.

Why a website and not an app: a visitor here for four days won't install anything. A link opens on any phone, shares in any group chat and gets found on Google. It installs to the home screen like an app for the people who want push alerts (iPhone supports this for home-screen web apps since iOS 16.4). If people come back weekly, we earn a native app later.

The pick

psst.

The top four ideas each do one job well. So instead of choosing one gimmick, they become one product with one promise: the friend who knows.

C becomesthe voiceEvery line reads like a text from your plugged-in friend. Plus an "ask psst." box.
D becomesNowThe home screen: the night map, live.
F becomesPlanPick dates, get a stay with an energy curve.
E becomesWallEvents, parties and pop-ups this week.
G becomesStickersA light layer that unlocks secrets.

Why the name. "psst." is the sound someone makes right before they tell you something good. It's the whole feeling in four letters, it works in any language and any city (psst. Miami, psst. New York), and the period makes it a statement. Names I weighed and passed on: Lowkey (close second, describes the spots but not the act of being told), Ahorita (funny, very Miami, but locks us to one city), The Plug (too many readings), Off Collins and Hotel Piña (my earlier rounds).

Your pineapple stays. It's the period in the logo. Each city gets its own emblem in that spot, and Miami's is the pineapple, the old symbol of a host who went to absurd lengths to welcome you. That is literally our job.

Brand

How it should feel: leaning in.

Three words: let in, warm, mischievous. Like the moment a bartender slides you something that isn't on the menu. Every design choice below serves that.

Logo

The word is set in a font that can stretch its width. The wordmark stretches out wide and snaps back, like someone leaning in to tell you something. You saw it at the top of this page. The pineapple sits in the period. In the app, the period pulses when something is happening near you right now.

Color: taken from Miami's real light

I skipped the Miami Vice pink and teal because every Miami thing uses it. These come from what the sun actually hits here. The app runs a day palette and a night palette and switches with the real time in Miami, so the color tells you the moment you're in. This page follows your device theme instead.

Photos, sound, motion

  • Photos: flash at night, hard sun by day, no stock. We shoot on phones with one recipe (4:5, direct flash after dark). It looks real because it is, and it's how nightlife actually looks. Every photo runs through the same processing so the whole site matches.
  • Sound: off by default. One soft whisper when you unlock a secret, nothing else.
  • Motion: springy and quick. Taps answer in under a quarter second, and only the signature moments run longer (under a second). Nothing blocks you, and reduced-motion settings get simple fades.

Six signature moments

  1. The night map. The home screen glows where Miami's energy is right now. Drag the clock and watch it flow from Grove breakfasts to downtown at sunrise. There's a working sketch below.
  2. Lean in. Secret moves are blurred. Press and hold, the logo stretches like a whisper, and it reveals. You get a few a week, and more by going places or sharing. Try it below.
  3. Storm swap. When the real forecast turns, your plan offers indoor swaps and a push tells you before the rain does.
  4. Flight-aware landing. Type your flight. psst. plans your first night around when you actually land and your last morning around your departure.
  5. The receipt. Any plan prints as a receipt-style image with times, moves and a secret password at the bottom. It's made for the group chat, and the password spreads the secrets by word of mouth.
  6. The wall. Event flyers you can drag and tear off to save. Promoters submit their own, which keeps it fresh for free.

The core unit

The move.

Everything in psst. is built from one object. A move is a place plus when it's best, what the weather allows, how much energy it takes and exactly what to do there. Getting this right is what lets the app answer "what now?" and Google can't.

The hard part

How we actually get the secrets.

Design gets people in. Content is the only thing that brings them back, and it's where every guide like this dies. So the content engine gets as much design as the interface.

Five sources of taste

  • You and five friends. Twenty moves each gets us to about 120 to launch.
  • Local hosts. Bartenders, DJs, chefs, tattoo artists, surfers each give their five moves under their name. They share their page, which is free distribution.
  • Promoters. A free "put your flyer on the wall" form. They want the reach, we get every pop-up.
  • Research. I comb local press, Reddit and Instagram for candidates and draft them as moves for review.
  • Users. "Went" and "still good?" taps keep moves honest without turning into Yelp.

The pipeline

Candidates→Claude drafts the move→You swipe yes / no (10 min a week, on your phone)→Open-or-closed check + date stamp→Live→Hidden at 90 days unless re-checked

AI drafts, a human with taste decides, and nothing goes public unedited. That's the line between a guide and slop.

Engineering

The stack, and why each piece.

The rule I used: pick the idea first, then the tool that serves it, and say what lost. I also changed one answer from last round, and the reason is below.

LayerPickAlso consideredWhy
FrameworkSvelteKitAstro, Next.js, NuxtLast round I said Astro, which is right for a magazine. psst. turned out to be an app: live clock, map, plans, saved state. SvelteKit ships a tiny runtime, has animation built in, and still prerenders every move as its own page for Google. Next.js is React-heavy and it's what your other sites use.
HostingCloudflare, on a personal accountVercel (your company account, off limits), Netlify, FlyHosting, database, image storage and nightly jobs in one place with a generous free tier. Fast everywhere. You create the account, I never touch Vercel.
DatabaseCloudflare D1 (SQLite)Supabase/Postgres, TursoWe have hundreds of moves, not millions. SQLite at the edge is fast and nearly free. Postgres would be power we don't need yet.
MapMapLibre + ProtomapsGoogle Maps, Mapbox, LeafletGoogle Maps makes us look like the thing we're beating. Mapbox costs per view. MapLibre is open source with full style control. Protomaps is a single map file we host ourselves, no per-view bill.
The glowCustom shader layer on the mapthree.js, deck.gl, PixiI dropped three.js. 3D isn't the idea, it would be decoration and weight. The signature is a 2D glow that moves with time, which is one small custom layer.
MotionGSAP + Svelte's built-in transitionsFramer Motion, Anime.jsGSAP is now completely free, plugins included, after Webflow bought it. Its drag and text-splitting tools power the flyer wall and the lean-in. Framer Motion is tied to React.
Small animationsRiveLottie, plain CSSRive animations react to input (hold, tilt, tap) instead of just playing back. That's what makes the stickers and the pineapple feel alive.
Ask psst.Claude API: Haiku 4.5 to understand the question, Sonnet 5.5 to draft movesA general chatbotIt can only answer from our own moves, so it never invents a place. Cheap per question.
Share imagesSatoriScreenshots, canvasTurns a plan into the receipt image on the server, sharp in every group chat preview.
Push, emailWeb push, plus a weekly emailA native appNo install needed. The Thursday email works for people who never install anything.
AnalyticsPostHogGoogle AnalyticsTracks the one number that matters (moves people actually took), with privacy settings we control.

Quality bar

  • The first screen loads in under 2 seconds on a mid-range Android on 4G. The map loads after the page is usable.
  • It works without the map: every map view has a plain list version, for speed and for screen readers.
  • Readable contrast everywhere, everything works by keyboard, and reduced-motion settings are respected.
  • Built city-agnostic from day one: city is just a field, so New York is content work, not a rebuild.

Risks

What could kill it, and the fix.

RiskFix
Crowded category (Places, Secret App, Spotted by Locals)Don't fight on "curated spots." Win on timing, events and plans, in one city, free.
Not enough content at launch120 moves before anyone sees it. The night map only shows once a neighborhood has enough moves to glow.
Stale picksEvery move has a check date and hides itself at 90 days.
Secret spots get overrunLean-ins are limited per person per week. Some spots stay word-of-mouth only.
Looks like AI slopReal photos, real names of the locals who picked each move, and no AI text goes live unedited.
Too clever to useEvery signature moment is a shortcut. A plain list is always one tap away.

Your calls

Five things only you can decide.

1. Name. psst. (my pick), Lowkey, or something you love more.
2. Open or invite-only at first. I'd start invite-only for 50 people. It protects the secrets and makes getting in feel like something.
3. A personal Cloudflare account. You create it (I can't make accounts), and nothing touches your company Vercel.
4. Five locals with taste. Names of friends who'd give us their five moves.
5. A small monthly budget. The domain and AI calls. A few dollars to start, and I'll show real numbers before anything is spent.
